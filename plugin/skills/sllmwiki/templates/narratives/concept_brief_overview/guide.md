# Writing guide: concept brief overview

## Audience and purpose

**Takeaway:** a reader who didn't know the concept has learned it by the end.

**Aim:** concise, precise, rigorous. Concise: the short version first, and nothing below definition level. Precise: the exact scope, in terms the reader can test. Rigorous: the definition from the source that defines it most exactly, with its nuances.

A reader meeting the idea for the first time, who needs to know what it means, why it matters and how to tell it apart from its neighbors, so they can follow anything built on it. It stays at definition level: how a particular system implements the idea, or a subtopic worth its own explanation, is another question, named under Where to go next.

## Voice and tone

Plain words, short sentences, present tense. Neutral where sources disagree: present each view, cited.

## Structure

- **Overview:** the orientation, through the key example.
- **Definition, in short:** a sentence or two, concise and precise, that sets the right intuition: what kind of thing it is and what sets it apart. Keep the scope exact (not "computers" when the definition says processes), cite what it rests on, and make it stand alone: it's what other narratives point to.
- **Definition, precisely,** when the field allows: the rigorous definition, quoted or framed from the source that defines it most exactly (often the landmark paper or the standard, not the most cited textbook): its conditions, the criterion that decides a borderline case, or its formal model. Then a brief on the subtle nuances it adds on top of the short version, and why each matters to the reader. When sources define it differently in substance, say how and why. When there is no rigorous definition, say so, and give the criteria the wiki works with, each cited; never invent rigor the sources don't support.
- **Key ideas:** what follows from the definition, the consequences a newcomer needs, each a short paragraph, in the order they build on each other; not the definition's conditions again.
- **Boundaries:** the definition tested on cases near its edge, which are in and which are out by its criteria; the neighbors it gets confused with; and what newcomers commonly get wrong.

## Examples

| Section | The key example's part |
|---|---|
| Overview | its scene: where the reader meets the concept, and what goes wrong without it |
| Definition | the short version in its terms; each nuance shown on it |
| Key ideas | where each idea shows in it, a sentence each |
| Boundaries | a variation of it near the edge, judged in or out |

## Evidence

For the rigorous definition, prefer the source that defines the subject most exactly: the original paper, the standard or the creator. Grade a source that defines the subject high relevance even when the rest of it is about something else. Textbooks and encyclopedic references serve the short version and the key ideas.

## Length

Short enough to finish in a few minutes, long enough to get the concept right: spend the words on the definition and its nuances. Everything beyond definition level goes to Where to go next.

## Checklist

- [ ] The short definition sets the intuition, with its exact scope, and stands alone.
- [ ] The rigorous definition comes from the source that defines it most exactly, and its nuances over the short version are explained; or the narrative says why there is none.
- [ ] Key ideas are consequences of the definition, not its conditions again.
- [ ] Nothing goes below definition level; deeper topics are named as next questions.
