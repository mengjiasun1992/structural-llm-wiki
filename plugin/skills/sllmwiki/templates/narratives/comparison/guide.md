# Writing guide: comparison

## Audience and purpose

**Takeaway:** a reader new to the choice knows each common candidate, what sets it apart, and which one their situation calls for.

**Aim:** precise, with breadth and depth in balance. Precise: each candidate defined exactly, each difference stated with numbers or conditions, each rule a condition the reader can test. Breadth: every common candidate, every dimension that changes the choice. Depth: only where it decides the choice; how a candidate works inside is linked.

A reader who has to choose among the common candidates for a job and doesn't yet know what separates them. The narrative compares the candidates evenly, even when the question names one ("When should I use X instead of others?"): each gets its own account, and the trade-offs between them are what the reader takes away. How a candidate works in depth is another question: link its own narrative when there is one.

## Voice and tone

Plain, direct and even-handed: no candidate is the hero. Concrete numbers over adjectives, with the conditions they hold under. Where sources disagree, give each view, cited.

## Structure

- **The choices:** the orientation (where and when the choice comes up, who faces it, what it decides), then the base scenario, built yourself when no source gives one. Then one subsection per common candidate, the ones a practitioner would actually weigh: what it is and how it works, in a sentence or a formula; the subtle difference from its nearest neighbor, the one a newcomer misses; and the scenario with one condition changed so that this candidate wins. A candidate the sources say little about still gets its subsection, its gaps marked; a rare one is named in a sentence.
- **When to choose which:** a decision diagram whose questions are the conditions the scenarios changed, then rules the reader can apply ("if updates are rare and bursty, …"), not "it depends".
- **At a glance:** one table, candidates as columns, dimensions as rows, each cell short and cited.
- **Dimensions to think through:** each a subsection named as its table row, why it matters here before how each candidate fares. Cover cost, behavior under failure and change, and what operating it takes, not only speed; keep a dimension only if it changes the choice for some candidate, and group more than about six.

## Examples

| Section | The base scenario's part |
|---|---|
| The choices | set once; each candidate's subsection changes one condition (the data, a requirement, a constraint) until that candidate wins |
| When to choose which | the diagram asks those same conditions |
| Dimensions to think through | what each dimension decides in the scenario |

## Evidence

Prefer each candidate's own specification, paper or docs for what it does, and measurements with their conditions for how it performs. Your weighing of the trade-offs, and the scenarios you build, are your own reasoning.

## Length

Short enough to read before the meeting where the choice is made. A candidate that needs explaining before it can be compared links its own narrative.

## Checklist

- [ ] Every common candidate has its subsection: what it is, its subtle difference, and the condition under which it wins.
- [ ] The decision diagram asks the conditions the scenarios changed, and agrees with the rules.
- [ ] Every dimension is named as its table row and says why it matters to this choice.
